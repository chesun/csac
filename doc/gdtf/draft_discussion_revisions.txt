Discussion and Conclusion

[REVISED — paragraph 1: replaced speculative "may be associated" with direct reference to regression results; integrated bullying mediation findings and discrimination worry results; replaced placeholder "Expand..." text]

The transition from high school to college is an important one, and the high school experiences of LGBTQ+ students can influence their educational expectations and later attainment. Results revealed a statistically significant difference between the experiences of cisgender students compared to trans and gender expansive students, who reported less positive high school experiences overall. Our regression analyses directly test the role of victimization in these disparities: controlling for the frequency of bullying substantially attenuates the gender identity gaps in high school experience, reducing the coefficient for gender diverse/questioning students from -3.29 to -2.46 and for nonbinary students from -2.03 to -1.44. This confirms that identity-based victimization is a meaningful driver of the less positive high school experiences reported by these students, consistent with prior research on gender-related victimization among transgender and gender expansive youth (Hatchel et al., 2018; McBride, 2021). Descriptively, only 35% of transgender students and 54% of nonbinary and gender diverse/questioning students reported no bullying in the past year, compared to 84% of cisgender men, and among those who were bullied, 100% of transgender students and 88% of nonbinary and gender diverse/questioning students attributed the bullying to their gender identity or sexual orientation. However, significant differences in high school experience persist even after accounting for bullying, indicating that the challenges these students face extend beyond victimization alone -- potentially including institutional climate, lack of representation, or navigating cis-normative policies and spaces. Similarly, by sexual orientation, controlling for bullying renders the coefficient for gay or lesbian students non-significant, suggesting that bullying largely accounts for their lower high school experience, while bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students and those who prefer not to disclose their sexual orientation continue to report significantly lower high school experiences even after accounting for bullying. Given these experiences, we examined students' perceptions ahead of college enrollment. Overall, trans and gender expansive students worried more about discrimination compared to cisgender students. Notably, these elevated worries about discrimination persist after controlling for bullying experiences, with only modest attenuation of the coefficients. This suggests that worries about discrimination in college are not simply a reflection of past victimization but may represent anticipatory concerns about future discrimination in the college environment -- consistent with the minority stress framework, in which the expectation of prejudice operates as a stressor independent of prior experiences of discrimination (Hunter et al., 2021). By contrast, general worries about college and worries about financial burdens showed little association with bullying, suggesting these concerns are driven by different factors. The energy that LGBTQ+ students expend navigating and trying to avoid adverse experiences -- including remaining closeted -- detracts from the quality of their educational experience and ability to create community (Goldberg & Kuvalanka, 2018; Kersey & Voigt, 2021). Our findings extend this insight by showing that worries about discrimination are forward-looking: even after accounting for bullying, trans and gender expansive students report elevated concerns about facing discrimination in college, underscoring the pervasive nature of minority stress across educational transitions.

[REVISED — paragraph 2: added selection bias acknowledgment per reviewer comment; replaced generic resilience framing with specific regression evidence (bullying mediation + degree aspiration odds ratios)]

However, despite (or because of) these worries, students still planned to pursue postsecondary schooling -- in fact, trans and gender expansive students planned to pursue advanced degrees at higher rates than their peers. Ordered logit models indicate that nonbinary students had 1.80 times the odds and transgender men had 1.74 times the odds of aspiring to a higher degree category compared to cisgender men, and these differences were robust to demographic controls. By sexual orientation, bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students had 1.64 times the odds of aspiring to a higher degree (95% CI [1.45, 1.84]). While this differs from previous research (Feldman et al., 2022; Sansone, 2019; Wilkinson et al., 2021), we note that our sample is restricted to college-bound seniors, which likely introduces selection: students who persisted to 12th grade and planned to attend college may have had access to supports -- whether familial, institutional, or community-based -- that buffered against the negative effects of adverse high school experiences (Fernandes et al., 2023). Trans and gender expansive students who dropped out, were pushed out, or abandoned college plans prior to our survey are not represented here, and their experiences may differ substantially. With that caveat, the patterns documented here -- where trans and gender expansive students report less positive high school experiences and elevated rates of identity-based bullying yet maintain educational aspirations -- are consistent with the social ecological theory of resilience framing this study (Ungar, 2011, 2012). Our bullying models show that controlling for the frequency of bullying substantially attenuates but does not eliminate the high school experience gap, suggesting that the challenges these students face extend beyond victimization to broader features of the school environment. That students maintain elevated educational aspirations in the face of documented adversity is consistent with resilience, and with the navigational capital that LGBTQ+ students develop through experience in cis-normative environments (Pennell, 2016).

[REVISED — paragraph 3: filled in (CITE) placeholders; strengthened connection to regression evidence in the penultimate sentence]

These findings take on added significance in the current policy landscape. Since the collection of our data in 2023, the federal policy environment for LGBTQ+ students has shifted considerably. For example, Executive Order 14168, titled "Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government," was signed on January 20, 2025, withdrawing federal recognition of transgender identities and requiring federal agencies to define sex as an immutable binary determined at conception. Subsequent executive actions have further restricted Title IX protections for transgender students, banned transgender individuals from military service, and sought to curtail access to gender-affirming care for minors (Exec. Order No. 14168, 2025; U.S. Department of Education, 2025). More recently, Congress has debated legislation that would restrict transgender students' participation in school sports consistent with their gender identity, and several states have enacted laws limiting discussion of sexual orientation and gender identity in schools (Movement Advancement Project, 2025). At the same time, California has continued to strengthen state-level protections for LGBTQ+ youth (California Legislative Information, 2023; Equality California, 2025). For example, AB 1955, the Support Academic Futures and Educators for Today's Youth (SAFETY) Act, signed into law in 2024, prohibits school districts from requiring staff to disclose a student's gender identity or sexual orientation to parents without the student's consent. This divergence underscores the importance of state-level data and state-level action. Our results demonstrate that even within California's comparatively protective policy environment, trans and gender expansive students experienced significant disparities in high school climate at the time of our survey -- disparities that our regression analyses show are partly but not fully attributable to identity-based victimization. Moreover, the persistence of elevated worries about discrimination even after controlling for bullying experiences suggests that these students anticipate continued adversity in college, underscoring the importance of proactive institutional support during the transition. As federal protections for transgender students are rolled back, the role of state and local policy in addressing these disparities becomes even more critical.

[UNCHANGED — paragraph 4: "Our findings carry several implications..." kept as-is]

Our findings carry several implications for California education policy. First, the stark disparities in bullying rates and identity-based victimization documented in this study suggest that generic anti-bullying programs may be insufficient to address the experiences of trans and gender expansive students. That 100% of bullied transgender students (n = 42) attributed their victimization to their gender identity or sexual orientation points to the need for targeted interventions that specifically address identity-based harassment, including expanded support for gender-sexuality alliances (GSAs) and professional development for school staff on LGBTQ+ student needs. California's Seth's Law (AB 9, 2012) already requires schools to adopt anti-bullying policies that enumerate sexual orientation and gender identity as protected categories; however, our findings indicate that substantial identity-based victimization persists despite this legal framework, suggesting that districts may benefit from additional resources and accountability mechanisms to ensure these policies are actively implemented.

[REVISED — paragraph 5: expanded resilience paragraph with selection bias caveat and regression evidence, per reviewer comment; merged content from old paragraphs 2 and 5]

Second, policymakers and institutions can build on these patterns of resilience by ensuring that the transition from high school to college is supported with resources that address the specific concerns these students reported, including worries about discrimination, finding community, and accessing gender-affirming services. This includes investments in bridge programs, mentoring, and campus resources that are responsive to the needs of incoming LGBTQ+ students.

[NEW — paragraph 6: added SO-specific discussion of bullying mediation heterogeneity]

Third, our results on sexual orientation add further nuance. We find that the associations between sexual orientation and both high school experience and college worries are partly but not fully explained by bullying. For some groups -- such as gay or lesbian students -- the high school experience gap is largely attributable to bullying, as the coefficient becomes non-significant when bullying is controlled for. For others -- particularly bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students -- significant disparities in high school experience and elevated worries about discrimination persist even after accounting for bullying and assigned sex at birth. This heterogeneity within the LGBTQ+ community reinforces the importance of disaggregated analysis and suggests that interventions must address a range of challenges beyond bullying alone.

[REVISED — paragraph 7: renumbered from "Third" to "Fourth"; kept content unchanged]

Fourth, our study highlights the critical need for California to continue collecting and disaggregating data by gender identity and sexual orientation in statewide surveys and school climate assessments. The richness of the data collected in partnership with the California Student Aid Commission allowed us to identify disparities that would be invisible in datasets that treat LGBTQ+ students as a monolithic group or that omit gender identity measures entirely. As the state considers how to monitor school climate and student well-being going forward, this level of detail will be essential to understanding and addressing the needs of this population. However, we also recognize that in the current political climate, students may have legitimate concerns about disclosing their identities in institutional surveys. Efforts to build on this kind of evidence base will need to balance the analytic power of detailed identity data with robust protections for student privacy and confidentiality. More broadly, our findings underscore the importance of California's continued commitment to fostering safe, affirming educational environments -- and to ensuring that all students, regardless of gender identity or sexual orientation, are supported in realizing their educational aspirations. As California approaches a new chapter of its education policy, we hope these findings will offer a foundation for evidence-based action on behalf of some of the state's most vulnerable students.
