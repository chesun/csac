High School Experiences

Students were first asked about their academic and social experiences in high school. We included a total of six Likert-scale survey questions on high school experiences, with each item ranging from -2 (very negative) to 2 (very positive), with 0 indicating a neutral experience (see Appendix B for high school experience survey items and summary statistics). We constructed an additive index by summing these six items, yielding a scale ranging from -12 to 12, where positive values denote an overall positive experience, negative values indicate an overall negative experience, and zero suggests a neutral experience.

We first compare average high school experiences by gender identity (Table 7). Cisgender men report the most positive high school experiences (mean = 4.27), followed by cisgender women (mean = 3.79). Trans and gender expansive students report substantially less positive experiences, with gender diverse/questioning students reporting the lowest scores (mean = 1.11). We plot these differences as regression coefficients in Figure 3 and find them statistically significant and robust to the inclusion of demographic controls.

We observe a similar pattern by sexual orientation. Relative to straight/heterosexual students (mean = 4.13), all other sexual orientation groups report significantly lower high school experience scores. Bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students (mean = 3.12) and students who prefer not to disclose their sexual orientation (mean = 2.91) report the largest gaps, while asexual/aromantic/demisexual students (mean = 3.68) and gay or lesbian students (mean = 3.55) report somewhat smaller differences. These differences are attenuated but remain statistically significant after controlling for race/ethnicity and parental education, as well as assigned sex at birth. While not causal, these results indicate that there is an association between both gender identity and sexual orientation and students' experiences in high school, which we consider in later analyses.

To investigate whether bullying experiences help explain the observed differences in high school experience by gender identity and sexual orientation, we estimated additional models that control for the frequency of bullying or harassment in the past year. As shown in Figure X, adding bullying as a covariate substantially attenuates the coefficients for gender identity. For example, the gap between gender diverse/questioning students and cisgender men narrows from -3.29 to -2.46 index points, and the gap for nonbinary students narrows from -2.03 to -1.44, though both remain highly significant. The coefficient for transgender women is no longer statistically significant after controlling for bullying (b = -0.80, p = 0.39), consistent with this group's small sample size. Across all gender identity categories, the R-squared increases from 0.034 (demographics only) to 0.072 (demographics plus bullying), indicating that bullying frequency explains a meaningful share of the variation in high school experiences.

A similar pattern emerges by sexual orientation (Figure X). After controlling for bullying, the coefficient for gay or lesbian students is no longer statistically significant (b = -0.32, p = 0.18), suggesting that the lower high school experience reported by this group is largely attributable to bullying experiences. The gap for bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students narrows from -1.11 to -0.72 but remains significant, indicating that bullying explains part but not all of the difference for this group. Further controlling for assigned sex at birth produces only modest additional attenuation. These results suggest that bullying is a meaningful mediator of the relationship between gender identity, sexual orientation, and overall high school experience, though it does not fully account for the observed disparities — particularly for bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students and those who prefer not to disclose their sexual orientation.


College Concerns by Sexual Orientation

We find a similar pattern when examining college concerns by sexual orientation. All non-heterosexual groups report significantly higher levels of general worries compared to straight/heterosexual students (Figure X). The magnitude of these differences is fairly consistent across sexual orientation categories, with point estimates ranging from roughly 0.5 to 1.0 units above straight/heterosexual students. Other/queer/questioning students report the highest level of general worries (mean = 4.58), though the confidence interval is wider due to smaller sample size. These differences remain statistically significant after controlling for demographics and high school experience, though the estimates are somewhat attenuated.

Worries about discrimination also differ significantly by sexual orientation (Figure X). All non-heterosexual groups report higher levels of worry about discrimination relative to straight/heterosexual students (mean = -1.39). Among sexual minority students, asexual/aromantic/demisexual and other/queer/questioning students report the largest differences, while bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students report somewhat smaller but still significant differences. These estimates are robust to the inclusion of demographic controls and the high school experience index.

As with gender identity, worries about financial burdens do not differ meaningfully by sexual orientation (Figure X). Point estimates are close to zero for most groups and confidence intervals generally overlap with the reference line, suggesting that financial concerns about college are not strongly associated with sexual orientation.


Bullying as a Potential Mediator of College Worries

To investigate whether bullying experiences help explain the observed differences in college worries, we estimated additional models that control for the frequency of bullying or harassment in the past year, alongside demographic controls and the high school experience index.

For general worries by gender identity, controlling for bullying modestly attenuates the coefficients but does not substantively change the pattern of results (Figure X). For example, the coefficient for nonbinary students decreases from 1.64 to 1.49, and for gender diverse/questioning students from 1.81 to 1.62, but all trans and gender expansive categories remain significantly higher than cisgender men. The bullying coefficients themselves are positive and significant, indicating that students who experienced more frequent bullying report higher general worries about college, though this association does not fully account for the gender identity differences. A similar pattern holds by sexual orientation (Figure X): all non-heterosexual groups continue to report significantly higher general worries after controlling for bullying, with only modest attenuation of the coefficients.

For worries about discrimination, the results are even more striking. After controlling for bullying, the coefficients for gender identity remain large and highly significant (Figure X). Transgender men maintain the highest level of worry about discrimination relative to cisgender men (b = 1.48), followed by transgender women (b = 1.22), nonbinary students (b = 0.97), and gender diverse/questioning students (b = 0.73). The persistence of these large coefficients after controlling for bullying suggests that worry about discrimination is not simply a reflection of past bullying experiences but may reflect broader anticipation of discrimination in the college environment. By sexual orientation (Figure X), all non-heterosexual groups likewise maintain significantly elevated worries about discrimination after controlling for bullying, with other/queer/questioning students (b = 0.71) and asexual/aromantic/demisexual students (b = 0.62) reporting the largest differences.

Consistent with the earlier findings, worries about financial burdens remain unrelated to either gender identity or sexual orientation after controlling for bullying (Figures X). The bullying coefficients themselves are small and largely non-significant in the financial burden models, confirming that financial concerns about college operate independently of both identity-based differences and bullying experiences.

Post-Secondary Educational Plans

To examine whether descriptive differences in academic plans persist after accounting for student demographics, we estimated logit models for intent to attend a four-year college and ordered logit models for highest degree aspirations, separately by gender identity and sexual orientation. We report odds ratios, where values greater than one indicate higher odds relative to the reference category.

For four-year enrollment by gender identity (Table X), nonbinary students were significantly more likely than cisgender men to plan to attend a four-year institution, and this difference was robust to the inclusion of demographic controls. Cisgender women were also significantly more likely to plan to attend a four-year college in both specifications. The odds ratio for gender diverse/questioning students was less than one in both specifications, consistent with the descriptive finding that this group was roughly equally split between two- and four-year enrollment plans. For the smallest gender identity subgroups — transgender men (n = 59) and transgender women (n = 20) — the odds ratios were greater than one, but estimates are imprecise due to limited sample sizes; therefore, we refrain from drawing conclusions about four-year enrollment for these categories based on the regression estimates alone and instead direct readers to the descriptive results reported in Figure X.

For highest degree aspirations by gender identity (Table X), we find that cisgender women had 1.63 times the odds of aspiring to a higher degree category compared to cisgender men in the unconditional model (OR = 1.63, 95% CI [1.49, 1.78]), increasing slightly to 1.73 with demographic controls. Similarly, nonbinary students had significantly higher degree aspirations (OR = 1.80, 95% CI [1.43, 2.26]), and transgender men also had significantly higher odds (OR = 1.74, 95% CI [1.10, 2.75]). Gender diverse/questioning students had elevated odds that were statistically significant in the unconditional model (OR = 1.58, 95% CI [1.02, 2.45]) but attenuated to marginal significance with controls. The odds ratio for transgender women was not statistically significant, likely due to the small sample size.

Results by sexual orientation were broadly consistent. For four-year enrollment (Table X), gay or lesbian students and bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students were significantly more likely to plan to attend a four-year college, and these differences persisted after controlling for demographics. The odds ratios for asexual/aromantic/demisexual and other/queer/questioning students were greater than one and significant in the unconditional models but attenuated and no longer statistically significant with the inclusion of demographic controls, suggesting that some of the descriptive differences for these groups may reflect compositional variation in race/ethnicity or parental education.

For degree aspirations (Tables X and X), ordered logit results reported as odds ratios indicate that nonbinary students had significantly higher degree aspirations than cisgender men (OR = 1.80, 95% CI [1.43, 2.26]), with the largest and most precisely estimated odds ratio among the trans and gender expansive categories; this difference remained robust with demographic controls (OR = 1.83, 95% CI [1.45, 2.30]). Transgender men also reported significantly higher degree aspirations in both the unconditional (OR = 1.74, 95% CI [1.10, 2.75]) and controlled (OR = 1.66, 95% CI [1.03, 2.67]) models. Gender diverse/questioning students had 1.58 times the odds of aspiring to a higher degree category in the unconditional model (95% CI [1.02, 2.45]), though this attenuated to marginal significance with demographic controls (OR = 1.54, 95% CI [0.99, 2.41]). Cisgender women had significantly higher degree aspirations across both specifications (OR = 1.63 unconditional; OR = 1.73 with controls). For transgender women, the very small sample size (n = 20) produced estimates that were too imprecise to support meaningful inference (OR = 1.19, 95% CI [0.55, 2.61]); while the descriptive patterns suggest that transgender students as a group report lower rates of certificate and associate degree aspirations and comparable or higher rates of master's degree aspirations relative to cisgender men, the regression models lack the statistical power to confirm these patterns for this subgroup individually. We therefore direct readers to the descriptive results in Figure 2 rather than interpret these coefficients substantively. In terms of sexual orientation, gay or lesbian students (OR = 1.53, 95% CI [1.25, 1.87]) and bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students (OR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.45, 1.84]) consistently reported higher degree aspirations across both specifications. The odds ratios for asexual/aromantic/demisexual students (OR = 1.52, 95% CI [1.17, 1.98]) and other/queer/questioning students (OR = 1.44, 95% CI [1.06, 1.96]) were significant in the unconditional models but attenuated and no longer statistically significant with the inclusion of demographic controls, suggesting that some of these descriptive differences may reflect compositional variation in race/ethnicity or parental education.
