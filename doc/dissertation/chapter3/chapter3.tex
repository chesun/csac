\section{\texorpdfstring{Introduction}{Introduction}}\label{introduction}

A critical choice students make in high school is whether to pursue a
postsecondary education. This choice is shaped by students' experiences
in high school (Kurlaender et al., 2019; Reed et al., 2023; Uwah et al.,
2008), which can influence their educational aspirations and
expectations (Bergerson, 2009)--- important predictors of later
educational attainment (Jacob \& Wilder, 2010; Sewell et al., 1969).
Moreover, research has long documented differences in aspirations and
expectations across student groups (Bergerson, 2009), including
race/ethnicity, gender, and first-generation status (Kao \& Tienda, 1998;
Mau \& Bikos, 2000; McCarron \& Inkelas, 2006; Solorzano, 1992; Trusty,
2002), with recent studies drawing on more expansive definitions of
gender identity and sexual orientation (Aragon et al., 2014; Feldman et
al., 2022).

Despite this, gaps are still present in research examining the
educational aspirations of different gender and sexual identities,
including intended field of study in college. Given that lesbian, gay,
bisexual, transgender, and queer (and/or questioning) (LGBTQ+) students
are distinct groups (e.g., Beemyn, 2012; Nicolazzo, 2017; Watson et al.,
2019), each of which likely experiences school and the college-going
process differently, it is important to interrogate the extent to which
college aspirations and expectations differ by gender identity and
sexual orientation. Moreover, as LGBTQ+ students frequently report
experiencing harassment and discrimination in high school---often
related to their sexual orientation, gender identity, and/or how they
express their gender (Kosciw et al., 2021)---this may impact their
specific academic aspirations and broader considerations about college,
as school victimization can lead to lower levels of school belonging and
self-esteem, high rates of absenteeism, and diminished academic
outcomes, including college enrollment (Aragon et al.,~2014; Heck et
al.,~2014; Kosciw et al.,~2013; Sansone, 2019). How differences in
LGBTQ+ students' secondary experiences may affect their educational
trajectories is also important to examine, particularly as federal
protections for LGBTQ+ students have been rolled back in the current
administration (Schultz, 2025). This work thus aims to examine
associations between gender identity and specific academic aspirations
(e.g., type of college, intended field of study) and college concerns
among college-bound 12\textsuperscript{th} graders in California. In addition, we
evaluate whether any differences in students' aspirations and
expectations may be attributable, at least in part, to differences in
high school experiences, including whether a student faced harassment or
bullying.

Therefore, this study explores the following research questions: (1) Do
students' college aspirations and expectations differ by gender identity
and sexual orientation? and (2) if so, to what extent are these observed
differences attributable to students' high school experiences? Drawing
on the results of a large-scale survey conducted in partnership with the
state agency responsible for administering financial aid in California,
we explore patterns in high school experiences and college expectations
(including academic plans, concerns, and excitements) to investigate
what LGBTQ+ students, particularly those that identify as transgender
and gender expansive, consider ahead of the college transition. We
specifically highlight trans and gender expansive students as they often
face distinct social challenges cisgender LGB+ students may not
experience at the same rate (James et al., 2016). By examining trans and
gender expansive students in addition to other subgroups of the LGBTQ+
community, we can analyze potential inequalities students may experience
and how this might influence the college transition process.

This study focuses on California students given the state's broad
diversity across demographics, geography, and postsecondary
opportunities. California is a particularly important context for this
analysis as the state has historically adopted more protective policies
for LGBTQ+ students compared to other states (e.g., the FAIR Education
Act, comprehensive anti-discrimination protections) and maintains one of
the most diverse student populations in the nation.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Defining Gender Identity and Sexual Orientation}{Defining Gender Identity and Sexual Orientation}}\label{defining-gender-identity-and-sexual-orientation}

Students use a variety of labels to describe both their gender identity
and sexual orientation (Callis, 2014; Galupo et al., 2015), reflecting
the fact that traditional binary labels (e.g., woman, man, heterosexual,
homosexual) are often constraining descriptors for the complex ways
individuals experience gender and sexuality \citep{hammack2022a}.
Moreover, research suggests students are constructing their own
identities through different labels (Watson et al., 2019; White et al.,
2018). Accordingly, gender identities beyond the established binary are
increasingly recognized and additional labels have also emerged to
describe sexual orientation apart from those traditionally defined
\citep{galupo2015a}. Although individuals continue to identify with
traditional binary labels, many students now use a more expansive set of
labels for both gender (e.g., nonbinary, genderfluid) and sexual
orientation (e.g., asexual, queer) (Beemyn, 2022; Hammack et al., 2022).
Additionally, not every individual may feel comfortable selecting a
label, or may be unsure about which labels to select, with \emph{questioning}
and \emph{unsure} commonly reported terms for both gender identity and sexual
orientation (Watson et al., 2019; White et al., 2018). In this study, we
utilize a novel dataset that acknowledges the spectrum of labels
students may use to define their identities outside a binary construct
and spotlight transgender, nonbinary, and gender diverse students in our
analysis to enrich understanding of these students' unique experiences
navigating the high school to college transition.

This paper is organized as follows: We first review prior literature and
the theoretical frameworks guiding our analysis. Next, we describe our
data sources, key measures, and analytic approach, followed by
quantitative and qualitative findings. We then discuss limitations
before considering implications for California education policy.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Theoretical Background and Prior Literature}{Theoretical Background and Prior Literature}}\label{theoretical-background-and-prior-literature}

The transition from high school to college is shaped by a confluence of
individual and institutional factors that can affect students'
educational expectations and later attainment \citep{kurlaender2019a}.
Individual differences, relationships, the school one attends, and
broader context all play a role during this critical period (Luh et al.,
2023; Pike \& Robbins, 2016). At the student level, attributes such as
motivation, self-efficacy, and academic performance moderate students'
educational \hl{trajectory (CITE).} Moreover, high school academic
performance is associated with one's aspirations to both two- and
four-year colleges \citep{desjardins2019a}, suggesting that students'
perceptions of their chances of admission and success also guide their
postsecondary ambitions.

Additionally, research has long demonstrated that various forms of
social and cultural capital play a role in shaping students' educational
pathways (Bourdieu, 1986; Dumais \& Ward, 2010; Merolla \& Jackson, 2014;
Plagens, 2011; Wells, 2008). For example, social capital, or the
resources available through one's social networks, shapes college-going
outcomes. Research shows that relationships with peers, family members,
and mentors can support students' acquisition of valuable information
about the college application and enrollment process and help develop
stronger college-going attitudes and behaviors (Alvarado \& An, 2015;
Hill, 2008; Perna, 2000; Skobba et al., 2018). Students that can
navigate the socially constructed norms surrounding the college process,
and whose networks provide access to academic and social resources for
this transition, are more likely to enroll in college (Choi et al.,
2008; Fletcher, 2015). As LGBTQ+ students often experience hostile
social environments in schools and at home, caring adults and social
support more broadly may be particularly important to the college-going
process. Beyond social capital, research suggests LGBTQ+ students living
in cis-normative environments exhibit particular strengths, or possess
several types of queer cultural capital (Pennell, 2016). This includes
navigational capital, or the ability to navigate schools and other
institutional systems that are not always designed with LGBTQ+ students
in mind. Here, students must learn to navigate systems and spaces (e.g.,
cis-normative school and state policies about gendered restrooms, sports
participation), potentially in creative ways, in order to access the
psychological, social, cultural, and physical resources they need for
their well-being (Pennell, 2016).

At the institutional level, schools shape students' expectations of
college and future educational attainment through the environments they
cultivate (Eccles \& Roeser, 2011; Uwah et al., 2008). A school culture
characterized by high values and expectations for learning is associated
with increased academic outcomes \citep{hattie2009a}, and school safety and
climate are also critical to engagement, belongingness, and achievement
(Osterman, 2000; Walton \& Cohen, 2011). As schools may be unsafe
learning environments for LGBTQ+ students, who often experience higher
rates of bias-based bullying and victimization \citep{day2018a},
LGBTQ+ students' perception of school climates may be inherently
different from those of their cisgender heterosexual peers. Moreover,
students' schooling experiences are mediated by stressors that underlie
societal conditions, including external (e.g., discrimination and
violence) and internal stressors (e.g., expectations of prejudice,
concealment, internalized stigmas). These stressors, heightened for
LGBTQ+ students, can affect psycho-social outcomes \citep{hunter2021a}, ultimately influencing educational expectations and the
transition from high school to college more broadly. For example, prior
research indicates that transgender youth often experience
gender-related victimization, particularly in secondary schools, which
may lead to higher levels of emotional distress and lower levels of
school belonging \citep{mcbride2021a}. Moreover, victimization, reduced
feelings of school belonging, and emotional distress can have a negative
effect on school attendance, academic performance, and expectations to
enroll in a four-year college for all LGBTQ+ youth \citep{aragon2014a}. Altogether, research suggests students' high school experiences
influence their future educational behavior (Gong \& Toutkoushian, 2024).

Our work is thus motivated by several interconnected frameworks to
understand how LGBTQ+ students navigate the transition from high school
to college. First, we draw on a newer model of college choice outlined
by Klasik and colleagues (2025) that builds upon prior models (e.g.,
Hossler \& Gallagher, 1987; Perna, 2006) to include expectations as a
critical component in students' predisposition for college. We examine
the ways in which high school experiences influence students' college
expectations (including academic plans, concerns, and excitements) in
the spring of their senior year and focus on an understudied and
marginalized group: LGBTQ+ students.

As college expectations are context-dependent, this work is also framed
by the social ecological theory of resilience \citep{ungar2011a} to
understand the potential variation in the college expectations of LGBTQ+
students, who must often navigate hostile environments and cis-normative
policies at school and within society more generally \citep{kosciw2022a}. LGBTQ+ youth that faced harassment or social exclusion in high
school may look towards college as a new opportunity to fit in, while
those who did not struggle as much may have other concerns. Similarly,
LGBTQ+ students that plan to live away from home while in college may
feel increased trepidation (i.e., leaving familial support) or, in
contrast, relief (i.e., escaping familial discomfort or hostility)
(O'Neill \& Conron et al., 2022). As college transitions vary across
demographic differences, including gender (Means et al., 2016;
Toutkoushian et al., 2018), students' identities concurrently influence
their lived experiences and expectations for their pathway to college
(Feldman et al., 2022; Fotopoulou, 2012). Therefore, it is important to
consider different elements of students' identities in addition to how
those identities are shaped by students' networks and environment. The
differences we observe in LGBTQ+ students' expectations for college will
reflect students' experiences and the ways in which they navigate their
own situated context.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Data and Methods}{Data and Methods}}\label{data-and-methods}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Survey Instrument and Sample Characteristics}{Survey Instrument and Sample Characteristics}}\label{survey-instrument-and-sample-characteristics}

In May 2023, in partnership with the California Student Aid Commission
(CSAC)---the state agency responsible for administering financial aid
programs for students attending public and private universities,
colleges, and vocational postsecondary institutions in California---we
administered a web-based survey to document the experiences of high
school seniors planning to enroll in college. The survey was distributed
to all seniors statewide that submitted either the Free Application for
Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) or the California Dream Act Application
(CADAA) for the 2023-24 academic year.{[}\^{}1{]} A total of \hl{XX
{[}number{]}} students were sent an invitation to participate in
the survey through CSAC; 10,221 completed surveys, including 9,230
respondents who indicated they planned to go to college, 170 who did not
plan to attend, 294 that were unsure, and 527 who did not \hl{respond to
the enrollment plans question.} Our response rate was \hl{XX
percent}. Students reported on varying dimensions of their high
school experiences; the support they received in preparation for
college; their academic interests and plans for postsecondary
enrollment; and their concerns about college. Detailed information on
gender identity and sexual orientation was also collected. Overall,
9,230 respondents completed surveys who indicated they were high school
seniors and intended to enroll in college that fall. After excluding
respondents who were missing information about gender identity (n =
1,708), sexual orientation (n = 1,749), race/ethnicity (n = 1,617), or
parental education (n = 1,647), our final analytic sample included 7,522
participants. Table 1 reports the demographic characteristics of our
analytical sample.

Compared to California's 2022--23 statewide 12th grade enrollment (see
Appendix Table A2), our sample is broadly, though imperfectly,
representative of the state's college-bound senior population on several
key dimensions. In terms of race and ethnicity, our sample mirrors
statewide enrollment, though Hispanic/Latinx students are modestly
underrepresented (49.8\% vs.~55.7\% statewide) while Black/African
American and Asian students are slightly overrepresented (7.0\% vs.~4.9\%
and 15.1\% vs.~9.5\%, respectively). A direct comparison of gender is more
difficult to draw given the state reports enrollment using only male,
female, and nonbinary categories. With that caveat in mind, our sample
skews more female than the statewide enrollment figures would suggest
(57.0\% cisgender women vs.~48.2\% female statewide), which may reflect
known patterns of differential college-going rates by gender. Given
these differences in how gender is measured, direct comparisons on that
dimension should be interpreted with caution.

Further, because survey participation was entirely voluntary, our sample
is self-selected, meaning that patterns in who chose to respond---and
how they responded---may not generalize to the full population of
college-bound seniors who filed a FAFSA or CADAA. Importantly, because
our sample is limited to students who completed a financial aid
application and intended to enroll in college, it excludes seniors who
did not pursue postsecondary education. To the extent that negative high
school experiences, such as identity-based victimization, contribute to
disengagement from schooling or lower college-going rates, our sample
may be positively selected, and the disparities documented here may
understate those experienced by the broader population of LGBTQ+ high
school students in California. We therefore interpret our findings as
descriptive of the experiences reported by this sample rather than as
estimates of population parameters.

To collect data on gender identity, we adopted the two-step approach
recommended by the GenIUSS group \citep{herman2014a} given its high
sensitivity and specificity in accurately discriminating between
transgender and cisgender respondents (Herman, 2014; Saperstein \&
Westbrook, 2021). This approach includes two questions: (1) What is your
sex assigned at birth, on your original birth certificate?, with \emph{male}
and \emph{female} as response options; and (2) What is your current gender
identity?, with \emph{woman}, \emph{man}, \emph{nonbinary}, \emph{prefer not to say}, and
\emph{other} as response options. Respondents who chose \emph{other} also had the
option to write in a response. Using these survey items, we classify
respondents into one of the following umbrella categories: \emph{cisgender},
\emph{transgender and gender expansiv}e (Robinson \& Cooper, 2024), and
\emph{prefer not to say}. Respondents who reported that their sex assigned at
birth aligned with their current gender identity (e.g., male and man;
female and woman) were categorized as \emph{cisgender}, while respondents who
reported their sex assigned at birth differed from their current gender
identity were categorized as \emph{trans(gender) and gender expansive}. The
Human Rights Campaign uses the term \emph{trans(gender) and gender expansive}
to describe ``the collective community of individuals whose gender
identities, gender presentations and expressions, and/or lived
experiences differ from those typically associated with the sex they
were assigned at birth'' (Robinson \& Cooper, 2024). Here, we adopt this
collective umbrella term to refer to all respondents who identified
differently than their sex assigned at birth while recognizing that
``trans'' and ``gender expansive'' are not necessarily distinct or mutually
exclusive identities, and that some may not use these labels to describe
their personal identity.

We further classify trans and gender expansive students into the
following sub-categories, which we utilize in our analysis: \emph{transgender
men, transgender women, nonbinary,} and \emph{gender diverse/questioning.}
For example, students who reported their sex assigned at birth as female
or male and who identified as a man or a woman were categorized as
\emph{binary-identifying transgender men} and \emph{women}, respectively\emph{.}
Students who selected \emph{nonbinary} or wrote in responses that included
the term ``nonbinary'' in their written responses were categorized as
\emph{nonbinary}, regardless of sex assigned at birth\emph{.} Students who wrote
in other diverse gender identities (e.g., genderfluid) are captured by
the term \emph{gender diverse/questioning}. Our analysis revealed some
significant differences between the experiences of nonbinary and gender
diverse/questioning students, which provides additional support for
these distinctions. Finally, students who chose \emph{prefer not to say} or
wrote in malicious responses (e.g., racial and gender slurs) were
classified as \emph{prefer not to say}. Among the trans and gender expansive
students in our sample (\emph{n} = 417), about 19\% identified as transgender,
64\% as nonbinary, and 17\% as gender diverse/questioning. Beyond
predefined options, students also noted 26 unique terms to describe
their gender identity, including \emph{genderfluid}, \emph{agender},
\emph{genderqueer}, and \emph{two-spirit}, among many others{[}\^{}2{]}\emph{.} We also
examined associations between gender identity and sexual orientation and
found that trans and gender expansive students were more likely to
select a non-heterosexual sexual orientation than cisgender students
(see Table A1 in Appendix A).{[}\^{}3{]}

To measure sexual orientation, we adapted the survey instrument
recommended by the Sexual Minority Assessment Research Team (SMART) to
capture the breadth of students' identities. Students were asked to
self-identify among the following options: \emph{lesbian or gay}; \emph{straight
(not gay or lesbian)}; \emph{bisexual}; \emph{asexual}; \emph{prefer not to say}; and
\emph{other}, which also included the opportunity to write in a response
\citep{badgett2009a}. The option \emph{asexual} was added to proffer more
expansive options for students. Within our sample, nearly one-quarter
(\emph{n} = 1,782) of students identified as LGB+, with most students
identifying as bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual (15.2\%), while about 4\% of
students identified as gay or lesbian, 2.4\% as
asexual/aromantic/demisexual, and 1.8\% as other/queer/questioning.
Similar to gender identity, students also used a variety of terms to
describe their sexual orientation beyond those provided, such as
\emph{pansexual} and \emph{queer}, while others used multiple terms or did not
want to label their sexual orientation.{[}\^{}4{]}

We also adapted questions on bullying and harassment from the California
Healthy Kids Survey \citep{austin2023a} to examine students\textquotesingle{}
experiences with identity-based victimization in high school.
Specifically, we asked students to report the frequency with which they
were bullied or harassed at school in the past 12 months, with response
options ranging from zero times to four or more times. Students who
reported being bullied at least once were then asked to identify the
reasons for their victimization, including race or ethnicity, religion,
gender identity, or sexual orientation.

A full descriptive examination of the rich diversity of labels students
used to describe their gender identities and sexual orientation in the
open response option, beyond those provided, is included in a companion
paper currently under review \citep{hurtt2026a}. Here, we
turn to how LGBTQ+ identities relate to students' high school
experiences and college expectations.

\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Data} \textbf{Analysis}}{Data Analysis}}\label{data-analysis}

We examine four main areas: high school experience; bullying and
harassment; concerns about college; and postsecondary educational
plans---including intended field of study, enrollment intentions, and
degree aspiration.

To measure high school experience, we construct an additive index
(equally weighted) from six survey items: students' ratings of their
high school academic performance, social experience, sense of belonging
in their school community, whether teachers and staff cared about their
future, the quality of college advising, and feelings of preparedness
for college.{[}\^{}5{]} All items range from -2 to 2, with -2 indicating a very
negative experience, 2 indicating a very positive experience, and 0
indicating a neutral experience. We construct the additive index using
these items given their internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.74).
The index for high school experiences ranges from -12 to 12, with higher
values indicating more positive high school experiences. To explore
differences in high school experience by gender and sexual orientation,
we regress the high school experience index on gender identity/sexual
orientation, first in an unconditional model and then adding controls
for race/ethnicity and parental education.{[}\^{}6{]}

We also descriptively examine rates of experiencing bullying and
harassment in high school, disaggregated by gender identity and sexual
orientation. For students who reported being bullied at least once, we
further examine whether they attributed the bullying to their gender
identity or sexual orientation.

To examine college concerns, we analyze the battery of 12 survey items
in which students were asked to rate how worried they were about their
upcoming college experience on a four-point Likert scale (0 = "Not at
all worried", 1 = ``Slightly worried'', 2 = ``Somewhat worried'', and 3 =
"Very worried"). Eight items captured general concerns: affording
tuition, living expenses, performing well academically, balancing work
obligations, balancing family obligations, finding community, living
away from home, and having adequate support for emotional and mental
health needs. Four items captured concerns about discrimination:
discrimination based on race, gender identity, sexual orientation, and
religion (see Appendix C and D for disaggregation of each item by gender
identity and sexual orientation, respectively).

We assessed the internal consistency of the 12 items using Cronbach's
alpha (0.80) and then performed principal component analysis (PCA){[}\^{}7{]}
to identify underlying dimensions of college worry. We retained
components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0, which identified three
principal components. We then constructed a composite worry index for
each retained component for every student as a weighted sum of the
student's responses to the 12 worry items, with weights given by the
eigenvector of the corresponding component. The first component, which
we label as \emph{general worries}, loads broadly across most items and
captures overall anxiety about the college transition. The second
component, \emph{worries about discrimination}, loads most heavily on the
four discrimination items. The third component, \emph{worries about financial
burdens}, loads primarily on the tuition and living expense items. Each
index is a continuous measure, with higher values indicating greater
concern along that dimension.

We then use regression analysis to explore potential differences in the
level of each type of worry by gender identity and sexual orientation.
We estimate three different specifications in our analysis: (1) an
unconditional model with gender identity/sexual orientation alone; (2) a
model controlling for student demographics (i.e., the full set of
race/ethnicity and highest parental education indicators); and (3) a
model that controls for student demographics and high school experience
index. Coefficient estimates from models (2) and (3) are very similar,
therefore we report only models (1) and (3). For robustness, we also
additionally estimate a model that controls for demographics, high
school experience index, and sex assigned at birth for differences by
sexual orientation. Results for this additional model are reported in
Appendix G.

We examine postsecondary educational plans along three dimensions.
First, we tabulate intended field of study by gender identity (Table 5).
Second, we estimate logit models for intent to attend a four-year
(versus two-year) college. Third, we estimate ordered logit models for
highest degree aspirations, where the outcome is an ordinal measure with
five categories: vocational/technical certificate, associate degree,
bachelor's degree, master's degree, and doctoral degree. The logit and
ordered logit models are estimated separately by gender identity and
sexual orientation. In each case, we estimate an unconditional model and
a model with controls for race/ethnicity and parent education. For
sexual orientation models, we additionally estimate a specification that
controls for assigned sex at birth. Cisgender men and
straight/heterosexual students serve as the reference categories. We
report odds ratios with 95\% confidence intervals and present predicted
probabilities from the ordered logit models as a stacked bar chart,
evaluated at sample means.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Findings}{Findings}}\label{findings}

\textbf{High School Experiences}

Using the high school experience index described in the Data Analysis
section, we first compare average high school experiences by gender
identity and sexual orientation. For summary statistics of survey items
over the entire sample, see Appendix B. For summary statistics of survey
items disaggregated by gender identity and sexual orientation, see
Appendix C.

We first compare average high school experiences by gender identity
(Table 2). Cisgender men report the most positive high school
experiences (mean = 4.27), followed by cisgender women (mean = 3.79).
Trans and gender expansive students report substantially less positive
experiences, with gender diverse/questioning students reporting the
lowest scores (mean = 1.11). We plot these differences as regression
coefficients in Figure 1 and find them statistically significant and
robust to the inclusion of demographic controls. While not causal, these
results indicate that there is an association between gender identity
and students' experiences in high school, which we consider in later
analyses.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Bullying in High School}{Bullying in High School}}\label{bullying-in-high-school}

As described in Data and Methods, students reported the frequency with
which they were bullied or harassed at school (response options included
\emph{0 times}, \emph{1 time}, \emph{2-3 times}, and \emph{4 or more times}). For students
who reported being bullied at least once, students were also asked about
the reasons they were harassed or bullied (i.e., race or ethnicity,
religion, gender identity, or sexual orientation).

Figure 3 presents the frequency of bullying by gender identity and
sexual orientation. Across all respondents, approximately 77\% of
students reported not being bullied or harassed on school property in
the past year, while roughly 10\% reported being bullied once, 9\%
reported being bullied two to three times, and about 4\% reported being
bullied four or more times. However, these rates varied considerably by
gender identity. Among cisgender men, nearly 84\% reported no bullying
compared to only about 35\% of transgender students and 54\% of nonbinary
and gender diverse/questioning students. Moreover, transgender students
reported the highest rates of repeated victimization, with approximately
27\% experiencing bullying two to three times and nearly 16\% experiencing
bullying four or more times --- rates far exceeding those of any other
gender identity group. Nonbinary and gender diverse/questioning students
also reported elevated rates of bullying relative to their cisgender
peers, with roughly 23\% reporting two to three incidents and about 10\%
reporting four or more.

Patterns by sexual orientation revealed similar disparities. While
approximately 82\% of straight/heterosexual students reported no bullying
in the past year, this figure dropped to about 61\% for gay or lesbian
students and 62\% for bisexual, pansexual, and omnisexual students.
Students identifying as other, queer, or questioning also reported lower
rates of no bullying (about 60\%) and higher rates of repeated
victimization compared to their heterosexual peers. Asexual, aromantic,
and demisexual students reported rates closer to the overall average,
though still below those of heterosexual students.

worries about discrimination that trans and gender expansive students

express excitement about the possibility of finding safer, more
affirming postsecondary environments.

\textbf{Bullying as a Potential Mediator of High School Experiences}

To investigate whether identity-based victimization helps explain the
observed differences in high school experience by gender identity and
sexual orientation, we estimated additional models that control for the
frequency of bullying or harassment in the past year (results presented
in Appendix I). As shown in Appendix Figure I1, adding bullying as a
covariate substantially attenuates the coefficients for gender identity.
For example, the gap between gender diverse/questioning students and
cisgender men narrows from -3.29 to -2.46 index points, and the gap for
nonbinary students narrows from -2.03 to -1.44, though both remain
highly significant. The coefficient for transgender women is no longer
statistically significant after controlling for bullying (b = -0.80, p =
0.39), consistent with this group\textquotesingle s small sample size. Across all
gender identity categories, the R-squared increases from 0.034
(demographics only) to 0.072 (demographics plus bullying), indicating
that bullying frequency explains a meaningful share, but not all, of the
variation in high school experiences.

A similar pattern emerges by sexual orientation (Appendix Figure I2).
After controlling for bullying, the coefficient for gay or lesbian
students is no longer statistically significant (b = -0.32, p = 0.18),
suggesting that the lower high school experience reported by this group
is largely attributable to bullying experiences. The gap for
bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students narrows from -1.11 to -0.72 but
remains significant, indicating that bullying explains part but not all
of the difference for this group. Further controlling for assigned sex
at birth produces only modest additional attenuation. These results
suggest that bullying is a meaningful mediator of the relationship
between gender identity, sexual orientation, and overall high school
experience, though it does not fully account for the observed
disparities --- particularly for bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students
and those who prefer not to disclose their sexual orientation.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Bullying as a Potential Mediator of College Worries}{Bullying as a Potential Mediator of College Worries}}\label{bullying-as-a-potential-mediator-of-college-worries}

To investigate whether bullying experiences help explain the observed
differences in college worries, we estimated additional models that
control for the frequency of bullying or harassment in the past year,
alongside demographic controls and the high school experience index
(results are presented in Appendix J).

For general worries by gender identity, controlling for bullying
modestly attenuates the coefficients but does not substantively change
the pattern of results (Appendix Figure J1). For example, the
coefficient for nonbinary students decreases from 1.64 to 1.49, and for
gender diverse/questioning students from 1.81 to 1.62, but all trans and
gender expansive categories remain significantly higher than cisgender
men. The bullying coefficients themselves are positive and significant,
indicating that students who experienced more frequent bullying report
higher general worries about college, though this association does not
fully account for the gender identity differences. A similar pattern
holds by sexual orientation (Appendix Figure J2): all non-heterosexual
groups continue to report significantly higher general worries after
controlling for bullying, with only modest attenuation of the
coefficients.

For worries about discrimination, the results are even more striking.
After controlling for bullying, the coefficients for gender identity
remain large and highly significant (Appendix Figure J3). Transgender
men maintain the highest level of worry about discrimination relative to
cisgender men (b = 1.48), followed by transgender women (b = 1.22),
nonbinary students (b = 0.97), and gender diverse/questioning students
(b = 0.73). The persistence of these large coefficients after
controlling for bullying suggests that worry about discrimination is not
simply a reflection of past bullying experiences but may reflect broader
anticipation of discrimination in the college environment. By sexual
orientation (Appendix Figure J4), all non-heterosexual groups likewise
maintain significantly elevated worries about discrimination after
controlling for bullying, with other/queer/questioning students (b =
0.71) and asexual/aromantic/demisexual students (b = 0.62) reporting the
largest differences.

Consistent with the earlier findings, worries about financial burdens
remain unrelated to either gender identity or sexual orientation after
controlling for bullying (Appendix Figures J5 and J6). The bullying
coefficients themselves are small and largely non-significant in the
financial burden models, confirming that financial concerns about
college operate independently of both identity-based differences and
bullying experiences.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Qualitative Findings}{Qualitative Findings}}\label{qualitative-findings}

The survey also asked respondents two open-ended questions: (1) ``\hl{What
do you think is the biggest challenge you'll face in college?'' and (2)

qualitative analysis of these data is beyond the scope of this paper, we

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{\emph{Social Challenges and Discrimination}}}{Social Challenges and Discrimination}}\label{social-challenges-and-discrimination}

Nearly one-quarter of trans and gender expansive students identified
social challenges as a concern, with many worried about making new
friends or whether they would make friends at all. A nonbinary student
described, ``Making friends has been a challenge for me in the past when
I had to go somewhere new and didn't know anyone, so it might take a
little while to get close with people.'' Transgender students also
expressed concerns over finding community once they entered college,
including worries they may not fit in, or may feel alone or overwhelmed
in a new social environment. These worries also included fears of
discrimination; multiple students noted concerns about transphobia,
coming out to others, and seeing kids from their high school. Moreover,
the most intense social concern came from one nonbinary student who
shared: ``I could die from hate crimes.''

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{\emph{Finding Friends and Community}}}{Finding Friends and Community}}\label{finding-friends-and-community}

In contrast, over 36\% of trans and gender expansive students were
excited about the social aspects of college, as students anticipated
meeting new people and building new friendships even despite some
apprehension. One nonbinary student expressed, ``I will be making friends
on my own, which is scary, but something to look forward to.'' Several
students looked forward to finding community, with one gender diverse
student writing: ``{[}I am excited{]} to be able to build a community for
myself where I truly feel that I belong.'' Additionally, students
anticipated meeting other students similar to themselves. One nonbinary
student explained, ``I'm most excited about the friendships I will form
with people who share the same mindset as me.'' Students also wanted to
meet people who shared their academic interests. As one gender
diverse/questioning student noted: ``I'm really excited to be part of a
supportive and engaging community\ldots{} {[}and{]} excited to be around people
with a similar drive for learning and an appreciation of education.''

Several students were also excited to attend a college where they could
access gender-affirming healthcare and housing. One gender
diverse/questioning student mentioned, ``I'm also going to be downtown
for college and I know that the college I'm going to is very LGBTQ+
friendly.'' Another nonbinary student explained, ``I'm also looking
forward to receiving certain medical treatment at my college's health
center that I currently don't have access to.''

Some trans and gender expansive students also looked forward to the
opportunity to leave their home environment. Students described a desire
to ``{[}get{]} away from home and a community that I was almost constantly
harassed in,'' ``leave my abusive household,'' and ``be away from home\ldots{}
{[}and{]} finally hav{[}e{]} my own autonomy.'' One trans woman added that
leaving home and entering college explicitly offered ``the ability to
transition my gender identity, as my parents are opposed to it.''
Together, these responses illustrate a pattern consistent with our
quantitative findings: even as trans and gender expansive students
report elevated concerns about discrimination and less positive high
school experiences, they simultaneously express hope that college will
offer safer, more affirming environments where they can build community
and access the support they need.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Limitations}{Limitations}}\label{limitations}

One limitation to consider in this work is that our sample is restricted
to students that indicated their plans to enroll in college in the fall
after their senior year. Our results therefore uniquely speak to a group
of students that made it to 12\textsuperscript{th} grade and made the decision to
explore college. As such, results do not include students who dropped
out prior to 12\textsuperscript{th} grade, had other plans, or for whom college seemed
out of reach---a population future research should examine in more
detail, particularly for LGBTQ+ students. We also wish to note that
students' plans can change---some students may not enroll in college
after the survey was administered, underscoring that expectations to
enroll do not always lead to enrollment (Klasik et al., 2025). Thus,
these results reflect a unique sample of intended college-goers captured
at one point in time. Future research on high school completion as well
as college enrollment and retention by gender identity is needed.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Discussion and Conclusion}{Discussion and Conclusion}}\label{discussion-and-conclusion}

The transition from high school to college is an important one, and the
high school experiences of LGBTQ+ students can influence their
educational expectations and later attainment. Our findings reveal that
identity-based bullying explains a meaningful share, but not all, of the
observed disparities in high school experience for trans and gender
expansive students. Controlling for bullying substantially attenuates
the gender identity gaps (e.g., from -3.29 to -2.46 for gender
diverse/questioning students), yet significant differences persist,
indicating that the challenges these students face extend beyond
victimization alone -\/- potentially including institutional climate,
lack of representation, or navigating cis-normative policies and spaces.
A similar pattern emerges by sexual orientation: bullying accounts for
the high school experience gap among gay or lesbian students but does
not fully explain the disparities for bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual
students or those who prefer not to disclose their orientation. These
findings are consistent with prior research on gender-related
victimization in high school (Hatchel et al., 2018; McBride, 2021).

Given these experiences, we examined students\textquotesingle{} concerns ahead of
college enrollment. Overall, trans and gender expansive students worried
more about discrimination compared to cisgender students. Notably, these
elevated worries about discrimination persist after controlling for
bullying experiences, with only modest attenuation of the coefficients.
This suggests that worries about discrimination in college are not
simply a reflection of past victimization but may represent anticipatory
concerns about future discrimination -\/- consistent with the minority
stress framework, in which the expectation of prejudice operates as a
stressor independent of prior experiences of discrimination (Hunter et
al., 2021). By contrast, general worries about college (e.g., tuition,
finding community, academic performance) and worries about financial
burdens showed little association with bullying, suggesting these
concerns are driven by different factors. The energy that LGBTQ+
students expend navigating and trying to avoid adverse experiences -\/-
including remaining closeted -\/- detracts from the quality of their
educational experience and ability to create community (Goldberg \&
Kuvalanka, 2018; Kersey \& Voigt, 2021), and our findings suggest that
this burden extends into the college transition: even after accounting
for bullying, trans and gender expansive students report elevated
concerns about facing discrimination in college, underscoring the
pervasive nature of minority stress across educational settings.

However, despite these worries, students still planned to pursue
postsecondary schooling - in fact, trans and gender expansive students
planned to pursue advanced degrees at higher rates than their peers. Our
ordered logit results quantify these differences: nonbinary students had
1.80 times the odds of aspiring to a higher degree category compared to
cisgender men (95\% CI {[}1.43, 2.26{]}), transgender men had 1.74 times
the odds (95\% CI {[}1.10, 2.75{]}), and by sexual orientation,
bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students had 1.64 times the odds (95\% CI
{[}1.45, 1.84{]}). These differences were robust to the inclusion of
demographic controls. While this differs from previous research (Feldman
et al., 2022; Sansone, 2019; Wilkinson et al., 2021), the schooling
experiences of transgender and gender expansive students in our sample
may include supports (whether at school or at home) potentially absent
to students documented in other studies. For example, students may have
had access to resources that can promote the well-being of trans and
gender expansive students, such as social support from peers, family,
and the community; school-inclusive policies; and extracurricular
activities, among others (Fernandes et al., 2023).

These findings take on added significance in the current policy
landscape. Since our data were collected in 2023, the federal policy
environment for LGBTQ+ students has shifted considerably, including
executive orders withdrawing federal recognition of transgender
identities, restrictions on Title IX protections, and efforts to curtail
access to gender-affirming care for minors (Exec. Order No.~14168, 2025;
U.S. Department of Education, 2025). At the same time, several states
have enacted laws limiting discussion of sexual orientation and gender
identity in schools (Movement Advancement Project, 2025), while
California has continued to strengthen state-level protections,
including AB 1955 (the SAFETY Act), which prohibits school districts
from requiring staff to disclose a student's gender identity or sexual
orientation to parents without the student's consent (California
Legislative Information, 2023; Equality California, 2025). This
divergence underscores the importance of state-level data and
state-level action. Our results suggest that even within California's
comparatively protective policy environment, trans and gender expansive
students experienced significant disparities in high school climate and
identity-based victimization at the time of our survey. As federal
protections for transgender students are rolled back, the role of state
and local policy in addressing these disparities becomes even more
critical.

These policy considerations are further complicated by heterogeneity
within the LGBTQ+ community. Our results on sexual orientation show that
the associations between sexual orientation and both high school
experience and college worries are partly but not fully explained by
bullying. For some groups -\/- such as gay or lesbian students -\/- the
high school experience gap is largely attributable to bullying, as the
coefficient becomes non-significant when bullying is controlled for. For
others, particularly bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students, significant
disparities in high school experience and elevated worries about
discrimination persist even after accounting for bullying and assigned
sex at birth. This heterogeneity reinforces the importance of
disaggregated analysis and suggests that interventions must address a
range of challenges beyond bullying alone.

Our findings carry several implications for California education policy.
First, the stark disparities in bullying rates and identity-based
victimization documented in this study suggest that generic
anti-bullying programs may be insufficient to address the experiences of
trans and gender expansive students. That 100\% of bullied transgender
students (\emph{n} = 42) attributed their victimization to their gender
identity or sexual orientation points to the need for targeted
interventions that specifically address identity-based harassment,
including expanded support for gender-sexuality alliances (GSAs) and
professional development for school staff on LGBTQ+ student needs.
California's Seth's Law (AB 9, 2012) already requires schools to adopt
anti-bullying policies that enumerate sexual orientation and gender
identity as protected categories; however, our findings indicate that
substantial identity-based victimization persists despite this legal
framework, suggesting that districts may benefit from additional
resources and accountability mechanisms to ensure these policies are
actively implemented.

Second, the patterns documented here -\/- where trans and gender
expansive students report less positive high school experiences and
elevated rates of identity-based bullying yet maintain elevated
educational aspirations -\/- are consistent with the social ecological
theory of resilience framing this study \citep{ungar2011a}. With the
sample caveats noted in the Limitations section above, the persistence
of elevated educational aspirations in the face of documented adversity
is consistent with resilience and with the navigational capital that
LGBTQ+ students develop through experience in cis-normative environments
(Pennell, 2016). Our qualitative data reinforce this interpretation:
even as students described fears of discrimination and hate crimes, they
simultaneously expressed excitement about finding community and
accessing gender-affirming resources in college, with one trans woman
noting that college offered ``the ability to transition my gender
identity, as my parents are opposed to it.'' Policymakers and
institutions can build on this resilience by ensuring that the
transition from high school to college is supported with resources that
address the specific concerns these students reported, including worries
about discrimination, finding community, and accessing gender-affirming
services. This includes investments in bridge programs, mentoring, and
campus resources that are responsive to the needs of incoming LGBTQ+
students.

Third, our study highlights the critical need for California to continue
collecting and disaggregating data by gender identity and sexual
orientation in statewide surveys and school climate assessments. The
richness of the data collected in partnership with the California
Student Aid Commission allowed us to identify disparities that would be
invisible in datasets that treat LGBTQ+ students as a monolithic group
or that omit gender identity measures entirely. As the state considers
how to monitor school climate and student well-being going forward, this
level of detail will be essential to understanding and addressing the
needs of this population. However, we also recognize that in the current
political climate, students may have legitimate concerns about
disclosing their identities in institutional surveys. Efforts to build
on this kind of evidence base will need to balance the analytic power of
detailed identity data with robust protections for student privacy and
confidentiality.

More broadly, our findings underscore the importance of California's
continued commitment to fostering safe, affirming educational
environments --- and to ensuring that all students, regardless of gender
identity or sexual orientation, are supported in realizing their
educational aspirations. As California approaches a new chapter of its
education policy, we hope these findings will offer a foundation for
evidence-based action on behalf of some of the state's most vulnerable
students.
