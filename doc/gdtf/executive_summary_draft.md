# Executive Summary

**Navigating the Transition to College: LGBTQ+ Students' High School
Experiences and Academic Plans**

Christina Sun, Alexandria Hurtt, and Michal Kurlaender \| University of
California, Davis

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This study addresses a gap in research on the diversity of experience
across gender identity and sexual orientation during the high school to
college transition. These questions are urgent given recent federal
rollbacks of LGBTQ+ protections, including executive orders withdrawing
recognition of transgender identities and restrictions on Title IX. As
other states have restricted discussion of sexual orientation and gender
identity in schools, California has strengthened protections,
underscoring the importance of state-level data and action.

We draw on a survey of 9,230 California high school seniors conducted in
May 2023 in partnership with the California Student Aid Commission
(CSAC), disaggregating results by gender identity and sexual
orientation. Our analytic sample includes 7,522 respondents.

## Key Findings

**LGBTQ+ students report substantially less positive high school
experiences than their cisgender/heterosexual peers.** On a composite
index of high school experience, cisgender men scored highest (mean =
4.3 on a -12 to 12 scale), followed by cisgender women (3.8), while
gender diverse/questioning students scored lowest (1.1). By sexual
orientation, all non-heterosexual groups reported significantly lower
scores than straight/heterosexual students (mean = 4.1), with
bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual students (3.1) and those who preferred not
to disclose (2.9) showing the largest gaps. These differences are robust
to demographic controls.

**Identity-based bullying is pervasive among LGBTQ+ students and only
partly explains the high school experience gap.** While 84% of cisgender
men reported no bullying in the past year, only 35% of transgender
students, 61% of gay/lesbian, and 62% of bisexual/pansexual/omnisexual
students reported the same. Among bullied students, 100% of transgender
students (*n* = 42) and 97% of gay or lesbian students attributed it to
their gender identity or sexual orientation, compared to 35% of
cisgender men and 28% of straight/heterosexual students. Controlling for
bullying attenuates the high school experience gaps but does not
eliminate them for most groups. (See Figure 3 and Figure 4).

**Worries about discrimination persist after controlling for bullying,
reflecting anticipatory concerns about future discrimination.** Trans
and gender expansive students report significantly higher discrimination
worries than cisgender peers, and these differences are not explained by
past bullying experiences, consistent with the minority stress
framework. Within the trans and gender expansive population,
binary-identifying transgender students report higher discrimination
worries than nonbinary and gender diverse/questioning students,
highlighting important heterogeneity that would be obscured by treating
LGBTQ+ students as a single group. Worries about financial burdens do
not differ by gender identity or sexual orientation. (See Figure 6)

**Despite less positive high school experiences, LGBTQ+ students
maintain elevated educational aspirations.** LGBTQ+ students planned to
enroll in four-year colleges and pursue advanced degrees at higher rates
than cisgender and heterosexual peers. Trans and gender expansive
students were also more likely to intend to study humanities and the
arts (25--31% vs. 11% overall). (See Figure 12)

**Qualitative responses reveal both fear of discrimination and hope that
college will offer a more affirming environment.** Nearly one-quarter of
trans and gender expansive students identified social challenges as a
primary college concern, including fears of transphobia. Yet over 36%
expressed excitement about finding community and accessing
gender-affirming resources.

## Policy Implications

- **Targeted anti-bullying interventions.** California's Seth's Law
  (AB 9) enumerates sexual orientation and gender identity as protected
  categories, yet identity-based victimization persists. Districts need
  additional resources and accountability, including expanded support
  for GSAs and staff professional development.

- **Individualized supports.** The variation in discrimination worries
  across gender identity subgroups reinforces the need for
  individualized supports rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.
  Bridge programs, mentoring, and campus resources should address the
  specific concerns these students reported: discrimination,
  community-building, and gender-affirming services.

- **Continued data collection efforts.** Our partnership with CSAC
  allowed us to identify disparities invisible in datasets that treat
  LGBTQ+ students as a monolithic group. This level of detail is
  essential for monitoring school climate, though it must be balanced
  with robust privacy protections.

**Caveats.** Our sample is restricted to college-intending seniors who
filed financial aid applications; the disparities documented here likely
understate those in the broader population. Small subgroup sizes (e.g.,
transgender women, *n* = 20) limit precision for some categories.
